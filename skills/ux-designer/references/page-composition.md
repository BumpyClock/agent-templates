# Page Composition

Read when: composing any full page or screen — the step between information architecture and visual system. Applies to product UI, dashboards, marketing pages, and content/report surfaces alike. This file is topology-level (what leads, what supports, how the page paces); aesthetic direction lives in `design-direction.md`.

## Frame the Job First
Before choosing layout, answer privately:
- Who opens this, in what context, to do or decide what?
- What is the single thing they must be able to do or understand?
- What earns their trust that it's true or working (evidence, state, proof)?
- What must stay available without dominating the first read?

Order sections by user need, not source or spec order. Every section must answer a new user question; merge duplicates, delete ceremony. Headings state the claim or the question, not the category ("Payouts arrive in 2 days", not "Features").

## Two Reading Speeds
Support both on any content-bearing page:
- **Skim path**: title, headings, key values, captions, and the closing state tell the story alone.
- **Study path**: exact tables, details, caveats, and sources preserved below for whoever needs them.

One home per claim: a detail table may preserve exact lookup, but a second summary, chart, or card must not restate the same answer at equal prominence.

## Reject the Obvious Layout
Privately name the layout the category reflex suggests (dashboard → KPI row + chart grid; landing → hero + three cards; settings → one long form). Reject it unless the material earns it. When the content admits multiple structures, compare two materially different composition hypotheses — different topology, density, and placement of the key content, not palette or component swaps — and pick the one that makes the user's job clearest.

Give the page one organizing move that belongs to this material and could not transfer unchanged to an unrelated product: a comparison geometry, a threshold, a sequence, a signature interaction. It must clarify the subject, not decorate it.

## The First Viewport Is the Argument
Not a masthead followed by setup. If the user saw only the first viewport, they should remember the central relationship, decision, or action — not just the title and the mood. (Marketing hero mechanics: `marketing-and-landing.md`.)

## Field, Not Stack
Compose the page as a field with one throughline, not a pile of equal-weight components:
- One focal relationship per reading moment or major section; a few supporting objects around it; open space that amplifies it.
- Pace the scroll: vary density and quiet while holding one visual grammar.
- Repetition creates rhythm only between true peers; repeated non-peers create template noise.
- Don't force materially unequal items into equal cells — rank, group, or give the decisive item more visual consequence so geometry matches importance.
- End deliberately: resolved state, next action, or open question. Never let the page just stop after a table or footnote.

## Empty Space: Deliberate or Defect
Open space must amplify a focal object. Large empty rectangles from an underfilled split, an orphaned third item, or delayed content are layout failures — reflow or rebalance. Judge whole transitions, not tokens: a valid spacing value beside a sparse row still compounds emptiness. Wrapped headings and labels must never visually bridge a gutter into the next column; widen the gutter, rebalance the content, or stack.

## Perceptual Tests
Run both before delivery (Gate 4):
- **Squint test**: at a glance, the dominant object is obvious and the reading path stable.
- **Text-mask test**: blur the words — hierarchy alone still communicates emphasis, grouping, progression.

If every block has equal weight, redesign before polishing.

## When It Feels Wrong
- **Busy vs loud — diagnose separately.** Busy = too much content: remove, combine, reorder. Loud = too much competing emphasis: reduce color, scale, weight, borders, surfaces, motion. Preserve one deliberate anchor either way.
- **Too safe**: strengthen one focal relationship through proportion, hierarchy, density, pacing, or placement; quiet the supporting content. Presence comes from commitment, not added effects.
- **Too thin**: improve selection, hierarchy, and explanation of what exists; leave honest gaps. Never fill a content gap with panels, borders, icons, color fields, or decorative visuals.
