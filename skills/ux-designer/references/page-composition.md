# Page Composition

Read when: a full page or screen needs detailed user-job, evidence, hierarchy, or composition guidance.
Use [Layout](layout.md) as the structural baseline.
Use [Design Direction](design-direction.md) for aesthetic choices.
These details apply to product interfaces, dashboards, marketing pages, and content or report surfaces.

## Frame the Job First
Before choosing layout, answer privately:
- Who opens this, in what context, to do or decide what?
- What is the single thing they must be able to do or understand?
- What earns their trust that it's true or working (evidence, state, proof)?
- What must stay available without dominating the first read?

Order sections by user need, not source or spec order. Every section must answer a new user question; merge duplicates, delete ceremony.

Use headings that make the task, topic, or claim easy to find.
For narrative or persuasive content, prefer specific claims or questions over generic section labels.
For settings or navigation, preserve useful category labels such as "Account" or "Notifications".

## Two Reading Speeds
Support both on any content-bearing page:
- **Skim path**: title, headings, key values, captions, and the closing state tell the story alone.
- **Study path**: exact tables, details, caveats, and sources preserved below for whoever needs them.

One home per claim: a detail table may preserve exact lookup, but a second summary, chart, or card must not restate the same answer at equal prominence.

## Choose a Composition

When a familiar layout makes the user's job clear and effective, preserve it.
Useful familiar options include a KPI row with chart grid, a hero with three cards, or a conventional settings form.
When a different structure could improve the task, consider alternatives in hierarchy, density, or content placement.

When a distinctive composition would clarify the material, consider a comparison geometry, threshold, sequence, or signature interaction.
Choose the option that best supports the task.
Distinctiveness alone does not justify a new structure.

## The First Viewport Is the Argument
Not a masthead followed by setup. If the user saw only the first viewport, they should remember the central relationship, decision, or action — not just the title and the mood.

## Hierarchy and Rhythm
Make content priority visible through emphasis, groups, and spatial rhythm:
- One focal relationship per reading moment or major section; a few supporting objects around it; open space that amplifies it.
- When density changes help the task, vary density and quiet while the visual grammar remains consistent.
- Repetition creates rhythm only between true peers; repeated non-peers create template noise.
- Don't force materially unequal items into equal cells — rank, group, or give the decisive item more visual consequence so geometry matches importance.
- For task flows, make the completion state or next action clear. For lookup or report pages, end after the complete evidence when no further action is needed.

## Empty Space: Deliberate or Defect
Open space must amplify a focal object. Large empty rectangles from an underfilled split, an orphaned third item, or delayed content are layout failures — reflow or rebalance. Judge whole transitions, not tokens: a valid spacing value beside a sparse row still compounds emptiness. Wrapped headings and labels must never visually bridge a gutter into the next column; widen the gutter, rebalance the content, or stack.

## Perceptual Tests
Use the checks relevant to the intended hierarchy during full-page visual verification:
- **Squint test:** Check whether primary tasks and content groups remain clear at a glance.
- **Text-mask test:** Blur text detail to inspect emphasis, groups, and progression.

Keep equal visual weight for true peers.
If unequal priorities appear equivalent, adjust the hierarchy before detail refinement.

## When It Feels Wrong
- **Busy vs loud — diagnose separately.** Busy = too much content: remove, combine, reorder. Loud = too much competing emphasis: reduce color, scale, weight, borders, surfaces, motion. Preserve one deliberate anchor either way.
- **Weak emphasis**: strengthen one focal relationship through proportion, hierarchy, density, pacing, or placement; quiet the supporting content. Presence comes from commitment, not added effects.
- **Too thin**: improve selection, hierarchy, and explanation of what exists; leave honest gaps. Never fill a content gap with panels, borders, icons, color fields, or decorative visuals.
